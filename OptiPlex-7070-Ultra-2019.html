OptiPlex 7070 Ultra 2019

The OptiPlex 7070 Ultra solved a problem that didn't have a product yet, a complete desktop PC hidden inside a monitor stand, eliminating footprint without sacrificing performance or upgradability. The concept originated from internal design development and was brought to market through a year-long program spanning concept, agency collaboration, engineering execution, and production validation.

The OptiPlex 7070 Ultra began as a thesis about a gap nobody had named yet. Enterprise desktop buyers were caught between two unsatisfying options. The all-in-one, which locked them into a display lifecycle tied to a compute refresh, and the micro form factor, which still demanded desk real estate and cable management. The space in between, a full PC hidden entirely inside a monitor stand, occupying zero footprint on the work surface, didn't exist as a product.
I developed that concept internally, grounded in competitive analysis across HP, Asus, and Lenovo's compact desktop lines, and a clear positioning argument. A modular design that decoupled the display from the compute without asking anything of the user's workspace. Dell serves a large segment of enterprise customers where office space is a premium and workers need reliable, minimal compute — deployed quickly and managed easily across large networks. The 7070 Ultra was designed for exactly that customer: an IT professional who needs a clean, fast deployment story and an end user who never needs to think about the hardware behind their screen. That idea became what Dell shipped as the 7070 Ultra, in 15W and 35W CPU configurations, competing directly in the enterprise compact segment.
Getting an unconventional concept through a large organization requires more than a strong idea. It requires conviction that skeptics can't dismiss. I worked side by side with an outside design agency on the Industrial Design and Engineering components throughout that process, not directing from a distance. What came out of that collaboration was a concept with real meaning. This was detailed enough to answer hard questions and allow executive buy in for this program.

One insurance customer used the device as secure way to allow their employees work from home with a "mobile" device. Insurance work needs to be secure and sensitive and they wanted their employees to be mobile but work securely at home and work. This allowed them to work in both places but not work in any environment where sensitive information could be seen by others like in a coffee shop environment using a mobile notebook. This allowed for a unique workflow for this company while allowing work from home.

Can you hide a fully functional PC in a display stand?

The market opportunity sat at the intersection of display adoption, chip miniaturization, and a generational shift in workplace expectations. The challenge was convincing IT decision-makers that a 15W zero-footprint design could meet their five-year compute and upgradability requirements.

Four enterprise desktop configurations mapped against ID quality, cord management, upgradability, and monitor flexibility. The zero-footprint configuration was the only option that delivered on all four, the strategic case that anchored executive review conversations.

Sketching ideas around how to mount it in a stand as well as use it as a stand alone device.

Physical prototyping was how we tested what the sketches couldn't answer. How the form sat on a stand, how the riser behaved in hand, where the proportions needed to change. These models were working tools, not presentation pieces.

Keeping it in the riser solved two problems simultaneously, routing cables cleanly through a single exit point while positioning the hard drive above the motherboard to optimize riser thickness and height. Every millimeter here was negotiated between design intent and engineering constraints. We were also wanting to align the design to our monitor family of products.

The compact desktop segment in 2019 ranged from $99 stick PCs to $1,599 premium AIOs. None occupied the space between, a monitor-integrated compute device with enterprise-grade performance and upgradability.

HP's approach split across two directions. Consumer friendly aesthetics with the Pavilion Wave and enterprise innovation with the Elite Slice. Neither resolved the cord management or footprint problem at the stand level. Notice they show no cords in this image.

Understanding a competitor's design strategy, not just their specs, shapes where you position against them. HP was moving toward audio-forward premium aesthetics. Asus was competing on price and storage flexibility. Neither was solving the workspace integration problem.
