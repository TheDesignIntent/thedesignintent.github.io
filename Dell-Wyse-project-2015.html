Dell Wyse project 2015

Dell WYSE Thin Client Program

When Dell acquired Wyse Technology, the product line that came with it had its own visual logic, proportions, surfaces, and details that belonged to a different design family entirely. My job wasn't to rebadge it, that was done immediately upon the acquisition. The real challenge was absorbing a foreign design language and replacing it, not patching over it with something that could live credibly inside Dell's portfolio. That meant harmonizing the programs into Dell products. Thin clients compound this challenge. They're treated as invisible infrastructure, commodity boxes that move data without demanding attention. Making purposeful, resolved hardware out of products the market often ignores as background equipment was the underlying design problem at every stage.

Over two years, working independently as design lead, I took three thin client desktop systems from a foreign acquisition to products that read as native Dell. They don't look like Wyse products that survived a rebrand. They look like Dell products that were designed to belong there. That's the measure of a real integration program, not that the logo fits, but that everything around it does too.

The process of this program. Studying how the architecture would be constructed in a way we can leverage the same chassis for multiple systems. We wanted this to be as efficient as possible. This program will live for a long time. Working alongside the architecture team, I studied how a shared platform could support multiple customer configurations without multiplying tooling costs. The modularization work was rigorous, not a cosmetic exercise but a structural one, asking which elements could be held constant across SKUs, where variation needed to live, and how those decisions would ripple through both manufacturing investment and the visual coherence of the line. That study ultimately informed platform architecture decisions even though the fully modular system wasn't built. Systems level thinking at that scale requires a designer to hold form and function simultaneously, every constraint on internal layout has an opinion about what the product looks like from the outside, and vice versa.
