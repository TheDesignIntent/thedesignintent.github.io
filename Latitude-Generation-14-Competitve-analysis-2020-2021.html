Latitude Generation 14 Competitve analysis 2020-2021

High level overview of how we stack up against our main competitors in easy digestible slides to initiate discussion of what we need to do next.

I worked with my team to build a competitive benchmarking framework not to generate research artifacts, but to change the terms of those conversations. The analysis ran across three areas: form factor, weight across configuration tiers, and CMF. This was mapped across the competitive set. Competitors included the HP EliteBook, Lenovo ThinkPad X1 Carbon, Lenovo ThinkPad X1 Yoga, Microsoft Surface, and Apple. Our team already knew the competitive landscape. We had to distill specific information required by the executives for us to understand the context of our market so we could make the best decisions at the time. Taking a dense matrix of specifications and material choices and reducing it to the exact figures that would matter to an executive who had many plates spinning in the air at any given time.
The dimensions and weight data anchored the form factor story. When I could show that our proposed chassis depth and weight targets tracked directly with competition, it reframed the conversation. The question shifted from "do we need to be this thin?" to "can we afford not to be." That distinction mattered in rooms with engineering and planning leadership present. The discussions were about dimensions, a lot of discussions around weight, and then could we meet the cost challenges in the relevant tier of products, given the procurement cost constraints of Dell.
The CMF data did the same work for materials. Carbon fiber wasn't a premium flourish; it was what the X1 Carbon had used to hold weight targets at a competitive price band for two generations. Showing that alongside our own weight aspirations gave the planning teams a market-grounded reason to engage on unit cost, rather than a flat default to the aluminum tier.
Benchmarking was the instrument I used to make the design position defensible. Not because the data made decisions for us, but because it gave our team something the planning team couldn't easily dismiss: evidence that the market had already moved, and a clear account of where we sat relative to it.
